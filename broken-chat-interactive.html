The situation

Earlier this year, a working professional was asked by their boss to get up to speed on the public comments filed about a proposed federal rule. When a federal agency proposes a new rule, anyone — companies, advocacy groups, lawmakers, members of the public — may submit a written comment about it, and this particular rule drew about 45,000 comments. The comments are collected in an online public file called a docket. Short on time, the professional opened a general-purpose AI assistant and asked it for help.

The conversation below is what actually happened — real, with light editing: we combined two sessions the professional had with the same tool, and shortened passages where the tool repeated itself. Shortened passages are clearly marked. Nothing was added that the tool did not produce.

Quick reference — six ways AI sessions fail, and five ways to recover

These are the names this course uses for the common ways an AI working session goes wrong, and for the moves that get you out of trouble. Four of the six failure types appear in the conversation below; the other two are included because you may meet them in your own work.

Failure types

  • Volume wall (often silent). The tool cannot handle the amount of material — for example, an upload limit accepts the first ten files and quietly drops the rest, sometimes with a warning that flashes and disappears. The danger is that it does not fail outright: it half-succeeds, then answers as confidently as if it had everything. This is the tool's fault. The fix is to change to a tool built for volume.
  • Didn't read your files. Asked to work from your documents, the tool instead summarizes coverage about them, while sounding as if it read the real thing. The fault is split: not giving it the sources is the driver's; not telling you what it actually used is the tool's — and that second half is the one that hurts you. The fix is to give it the documents, then confirm it used them.
  • Too-thin ask, too-thin answer. The tool was given nothing to work from — no sources, no scope, no definition of done — or it hands back three bland bullets for a two-hundred-page pile. This is the driver's fault. The fix is to reframe the ask: who it is for, what format, what sources, and what "done" looks like.
  • Arguing loop. You challenge the tool, it apologizes, and the same answer comes back reshuffled. Continuing the argument is the driver's fault. The fix is to start a new chat.
  • Refusal / punt. The tool declines the task — "I can't advise on that." This is the tool working as designed. The fix is to keep that judgment human.
  • False precision under pressure. Pushed for specifics, the tool invents section numbers, quotations, or citations. This is the tool's fault. The fix is to verify every specific before you use it.

The five recovery moves (try them in this order — each is cheaper than the next)

  • Rephrase. Reframe the ask: say who the work is for, what format you need, and what to include.
  • Re-source. Give the tool the actual documents instead of hoping it finds them — then confirm it used them.
  • Restart. Open a fresh chat. Once a session starts arguing, more messages make it worse.
  • Re-tool. Switch to a tool that was built for the job.
  • Reclaim. Take the task back. Some calls were never the tool's to make.
